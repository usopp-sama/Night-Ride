# 01 — Product promise and simple story

## One-sentence pitch

Drive, repair, explore, and make a home anywhere across a seeded night world; beyond the peaceful sea crossing, discover why independent drivers are disappearing and bring a person and a second car home.

## Player experience

Most nights are quiet. The player chooses a road, a destination, or no destination. The car is a personal machine: a place to sit, hear the engine, listen to radio, carry tools, and remember earlier trips. A found viewpoint or a smooth journey can be a complete session. Longer-term players build camps and workshops, restore cars, map routes, help people, and eventually follow the main story.

The game has four rhythms:

| Rhythm | What happens | Emotional purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cruise | Drive with music/radio, weather, and road texture | Peace and familiarity |
| Stop | Park, inspect, repair, talk, trade, camp, photograph | Ownership and attachment |
| Expedition | Prepare for longer, less-known routes | Accomplishment through judgment |
| Story | Help specific people as the Night Line disappears | Rising stakes and payoff |

The player can remain in the first three rhythms indefinitely in Marren.

## The world

Three fictional countries share a coherent procedural world. Names are working names and should be tested for tone before localization.

- **Marren (Country 1):** an island or island-like landmass across open sea. Safe roads, small towns, independent mechanics, camps, and freedom to roam. The player spawns here. The ferry port sits at a predictable *route distance* from spawn, in a seed-dependent direction.
- **Hest (Country 2):** another country reached by ferry. Independent night routes coexist uneasily with a licensed transport system. The player can return home at any time before the Soren commitment.
- **Soren (Country 3):** connected to Hest by road. Its transport system is centralized; independent cars and workshops have lost practical rights. The final story takes place here.

Countries should feel different through road language, settlement services, traffic, signs, radio, part supply, and architecture. Geography and politics must remain plausible across seeds; generated variation changes where things are, not the story's meaning.

## The Night Line

The Night Line is an informal network of drivers, mechanics, fuel stops, workshops, and hosts who help people travel after dark. It is a social practice, not an organization with a secret headquarters. Marren keeps it openly. Hest still has pieces of it. Soren's transport authority has largely displaced it.

Each meaningful story task should show what the network does for a specific person. A part arriving lets a bus run. A repaired van lets a family visit another town. A reopened stop means drivers have somewhere to rest. This makes the larger conflict legible through the player's car.

## Simple plot

1. **Peace:** The player lives in Marren with an old but reliable car. They drive, explore, modify it, camp, trade, and make their own routes. They can hear fragments about Elias, the person who taught them to drive and left years ago, but there is no urgent main quest.
2. **Curiosity:** Ordinary discoveries—an old repair mark, a radio phrase, workshop notes, or a traveler's account—point toward Hest. The ferry makes crossing an intentional journey. The story begins after arrival and an encounter at a Hest night stop.
3. **Discovery:** In Hest, the player learns that an authority spanning Hest and Soren is replacing independent routes with licensed transport. Remote places lose fuel, parts, and night service. Elias helped the remaining drivers preserve a final route; he and his car are now in Soren.
4. **Choice:** The player helps named drivers and mechanics in Hest. They restore stops, move parts, repair vehicles, and find Elias's route records. They may return to Marren by ferry as often as they wish.
5. **Commitment:** Entering Soren starts a warned final chapter. The player's unlicensed car cannot return through the controlled road exit while the transport authority holds Elias and his car. The player can keep exploring reachable Soren locations and resume saves there; “lock” means no trip home until resolution, not a forced uninterrupted mission.
6. **Escalation:** Workshops and routes the player relied on are closed; Elias's car is moved toward conversion into a fleet vehicle. The authority's director believes central licensing prevents breakdowns and waste, giving the conflict a human argument.
7. **Final night:** The player prepares both cars and helps independent drivers use a route made possible by their earlier choices. Road access closes in stages. The climax is a tense driving and repair sequence that demands route judgment, car care, and help from allies, not a cutscene-only victory.
8. **Return:** The two cars cross out of Soren and travel through Hest to Marren at dawn. Elias returns. The independent route survives and gains limited legal recognition; the larger system is not magically erased.
9. **After credits:** The world reopens. Players can drive all three countries, restore more vehicles and stops, continue building, and revisit the places their decisions changed.

## Character roles

- **Player:** defined mainly by actions and car choices; dialogue choices should not overwrite the player's calm self-directed identity.
- **Elias:** mentor and missing driver; has agency and made imperfect decisions. Their car is a tangible emotional anchor. The name, relationship, and pronouns are provisional presentation choices.
- **Mara Venn:** Soren transport director. She has real evidence for safety improvements but unacceptable methods of control. Give her scenes where the player can understand her argument without agreeing.
- **Hest anchor mechanic:** a recurring person who makes the conflict concrete, shows the first closed stop, and later helps prepare the final run.
- **Marren anchor:** a friend/neighbor who represents home without demanding the player return to a fixed garage.

## What success feels like

The closing image is two tired cars returning to Marren at dawn. The player knows the road because they have driven it before. Credits follow the emotional resolution, then the game loads an after-story world where the journey's consequences are visible.

The story cannot rely on every player discovering every clue or building every camp. Required story beats have safe defaults; optional preparation changes the final route, allies, and dialogue. See [07](07-story-implementation.md).
